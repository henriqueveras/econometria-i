Computation

  • The idea, in one line. Pick the parameter under which the data you saw are least surprising. 7 heads in 10 tosses \(\Rightarrow\hat p=0.7\), the sample frequency — that rule is maximum likelihood (maximise plausibility, not minimise error).
  • What the maximum does. At the peak the slope — the score — is zero, and that zero makes the model match the data (fitted probability \(=\) observed frequency), which is why it pins \(\btheta\) down.
  • Why pay to assume the whole density? Two returns — efficiency (smallest possible SE) and reach (binary outcomes, where OLS has no coherent model) — for a stronger assumption. The axis: efficiency vs. robustness.
  • Same architecture, third member. OLS minimises SSR, GMM minimises \(J\), MLE maximises \(\ell\); the score\(=\bzero\) is Lecture 11’s Z-estimator. Destination: Logit/Probit.
◆What is the DGP?—What is the DGP, here?

A fully specified probability model: the data \(\mathbf{d}_i\) are i.i.d. from a known density \(f(\mathbf{d}\mid\btheta_0)\), only \(\btheta_0\) unknown. Committing to the whole distribution — not just one moment — is what buys efficiency and what costs a stronger assumption.

Part I · From minimising error to maximising likelihood

The likelihood and the MLE

Each observation carries a plausibility \(f(\mathbf{d}_i\mid\btheta)\); by i.i.d. the joint plausibility of the whole sample factors into the product — the likelihood. Logs and a \(1/n\) turn it into the average we maximise (same maximiser, since \(\log\) is monotone): \[L_n(\btheta)=\prod_{i=1}^n f(\mathbf{d}_i\mid\btheta), \qquad \hat\btheta=\argmax_{\btheta}\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log f(\mathbf{d}_i\mid\btheta).\]

  • The likelihood is a product. \(L_n(\btheta)=\prod_i f(\mathbf{d}_i\mid\btheta)\) — the joint density of the sample, read backwards: the data are fixed, \(\btheta\) is the unknown. The i.i.d. assumption is exactly what lets the joint factor into the product of marginals.
  • A different philosophy of estimation. OLS/GMM minimise a loss (how far the model sits from the data); MLE maximises a plausibility (how probable what we saw is). Not the same recipe with a flipped sign — a different question.
  • Why the log? It is monotone, so it does not move the \(\argmax\); but it turns \(\prod\) into \(\sum\), so (i) derivatives become sums, hence tractable, and (ii) the LLN/CLT can act on an average. Pure convenience — the definition is the product.
  • Why the \(\tfrac1n\)? It makes the sum a sample average, so \(\ell_n\) is an M-estimator criterion — the very shape of \(\argmin\) SSR (OLS) and \(\argmin J\) (GMM). Only the criterion changed: squared error → log-density.

The score equation is a Z-estimator

Differentiate the criterion. Define the score — the gradient of the log-density of one observation — and set the sample average of scores to zero: \[\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{d},\btheta)=\frac{\partial\log f(\mathbf{d}\mid\btheta)}{\partial\btheta}, \qquad \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{d}_i,\hat\btheta)=\bzero.\]

  • The FOC of \(\argmax\ell_n\) is \(\tfrac1n\sum_i\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{d}_i,\hat\btheta)=\bzero\) — the sample average of the score is zero.
  • This is \(\tfrac1n\sum_i\boldsymbol\psi(\mathbf{d}_i,\hat\btheta)=\bzero\) with \(\boldsymbol\psi=\mathbf{s}\): a Z-estimator (Lecture 11).
  • MLE solves a moment equation — the moment is the score.
🔗Connection—Back to Lecture 11's closing box

GMM’s own first-order condition was \(\bG'\bW\,\bar{\mathbf{g}}_n(\hat\btheta)=\bzero\) — a moment equation we called a Z-estimator, and promised reached forward to MLE. Here is the payoff: the MLE is the Z-estimator whose moment is the model’s score. OLS, GMM and MLE are the same kind of object; they differ only in which function is set to average zero.

Part II · What the score buys: information and efficiency

How much do the data pin \(\btheta\) down? Fisher information

\(\hat\btheta\) is a point — how tightly is it pinned? Look at the shape of the likelihood peak. A sharp peak means nudging \(\btheta\) off \(\hat\btheta\) makes the data far less likely (they locate \(\btheta\) precisely); a flat peak means they barely do. That average sharpness is the Fisher information, and it has two equal readings: \[\mathcal I(\btheta_0)\ \equiv\ \underbrace{-\,\E[\bH]}_{\text{curvature of }\ell}\ =\ \underbrace{\Var(\mathbf{s})=\E[\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}']}_{\text{jitter of the score}}\ \succeq\ \bzero.\]

  • The score is mean-zero at the truth, \(\E_{\btheta_0}[\mathbf{s}]=\bzero\): at \(\btheta_0\) there is no systematic uphill, so aiming at “score \(=\bzero\)” aims at the truth (and the CLT centres at zero). It is a valid moment, as in GMM.
  • Fisher information = sharpness of the peak — two faces: how concave \(\ell\) is (\(-\E[\bH]\)) and how much the score jitters across samples (\(\Var(\mathbf{s})\)). The information equality says they are the same number \(\mathcal I\).
  • Both facts are just \(\int f\,d\mathbf{d}=1\) differentiated — once gives \(\E[\mathbf{s}]=\bzero\), twice gives the equality.
  • Why you care: more information \(\Rightarrow\) tighter estimate — the SE will be \(1/\sqrt{n\mathcal I}\) (next slide). Holds only under correct specification, else the two readings diverge (robust SEs).

Asymptotic normality: the Cramér–Rao bound

Linearise the score around \(\btheta_0\), substitute the two limits (LLN for the Hessian, CLT for the score), and the information equality collapses the general M-estimator sandwich to a single inverse: \[\sqrt n(\hat\btheta-\btheta_0)\ \dto\ \Normal\!\big(\bzero,\ \mathcal I(\btheta_0)^{-1}\big).\]

  • Error \(=\) score-noise \(\div\) curvature. Linearising gives \(\sqrt n(\hat\btheta-\btheta_0)\approx-\bar\bH_n^{-1}\sqrt n\,\bar{\mathbf{s}}_n(\btheta_0)\), with \(\bar\bH_n\pto-\mathcal I\) (LLN) and \(\sqrt n\,\bar{\mathbf{s}}_n\dto\Normal(\bzero,\mathcal I)\) (CLT) — divide the score’s noise by the peak’s sharpness.
  • The SE you read off: \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}=\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal I}^{-1}/n}\), with \(\hat{\mathcal I}\) from the Hessian or the outer product of scores (OPG/BHHH) — equal by the information equality. Sharp peak (big \(\mathcal I\)) \(\Rightarrow\) small SE.
  • \(\mathcal I^{-1}\) is the Cramér–Rao bound — the floor on the variance of any regular unbiased estimator, fixed by the sample’s information. The MLE hits it \(\Rightarrow\) efficient: Gauss–Markov’s promise, now among all estimators, not just linear.
🔗Connection—The same collapse as the optimal GMM weight

In Lecture 11 the GMM variance collapsed to \((\bG'\bS^{-1}\bG)^{-1}\) exactly when \(\bW=\bS^{-1}\) — the right metric hit the bound. MLE is the same story with one model: the true density is its own weight, so it attains \(\mathcal I^{-1}\). Free corollary: with normal errors the MLE of \(\bbeta\) is OLS (its normal equations \(\mathbf{X}'\be=\bzero\) are a score) — Gauss–Markov efficiency is this bound in disguise.

Part III · Binary choice: the leading application

Why a 0/1 outcome calls for the likelihood

The outcome is now \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) — and this is where MLE earns its keep. Parts I–II showed MLE buys efficiency by using the whole density; a binary outcome forces that, because there is no continuous “error to minimise”, yet the density is known exactly: given \(\mathbf{x}_i\), \(y_i\) is Bernoulli with \(p(\mathbf{x}_i)=P(y_i=1\mid\mathbf{x}_i)\). Model that probability by sending the index through a bounded, increasing \(F\): \[P(y_i=1\mid\mathbf{x}_i)=F(\mathbf{x}_i'\bbeta)\in[0,1],\] with Logit (\(F=\Lambda\)) or Probit (\(F=\Phi\)) — two symmetric CDFs that barely differ in fit, only in error scale.

  • Why not just run OLS — the LPM, \(P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}'\bbeta\)? The textbook complaints are weak in our setting: fitted values outside \([0,1]\) only bite for prediction (never our aim), and heteroskedasticity we’ve treated as the norm — robust SEs — all course.
  • The real problem is the shape. A straight line imposes a constant effect \(\beta_j\) — but a probability cannot keep responding at the same rate as it nears \(0\) or \(1\); the effect must taper at the extremes. The LPM ignores this and the Bernoulli density it was handed — forgoing Part II’s efficiency. A CDF \(F\) fixes both.
  • Where \(F\) comes from — the structural reading. A latent propensity \(y_i^{*}=\mathbf{x}_i'\bbeta+u_i\) (utility, willingness to pay) seen only as \(y_i=\mathbf 1\{y_i^{*}>0\}\) gives \(P(y_i=1\mid\mathbf{x}_i)=P(u_i>-\mathbf{x}_i'\bbeta)=F(\mathbf{x}_i'\bbeta)\) by symmetry — the linear model through a slit that shows only \(\operatorname{sign}(y^{*})\), which is what leaves only \(\bbeta/\sigma_u\) identified (next slide).

The likelihood and its score

Here Part I’s architecture cashes out: we finally have a concrete, known density to plug in. Each observation is Bernoulli — the model assigns \(F_i=F(\mathbf{x}_i'\bbeta)\) to \(y_i=1\) and \(1-F_i\) to \(y_i=0\), written together as \(F_i^{y_i}(1-F_i)^{1-y_i}\). By i.i.d. the likelihood is the product, the log a sum, and the score (chain rule, \(f=F'\)) a weighted residual: \[L_n(\bbeta)=\prod_i F_i^{\,y_i}(1-F_i)^{1-y_i}, \qquad \ell_n(\bbeta)=\frac1n\sum_i\big[y_i\log F_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-F_i)\big],\] \[\mathbf{s}_i=\frac{f(\mathbf{x}_i'\bbeta)}{F_i(1-F_i)}\big(y_i-F_i\big)\mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \frac1n\sum_i\mathbf{s}_i=\bzero .\]

  • Same construction as Part I: i.i.d. Bernoulli \(\Rightarrow\) product \(\Rightarrow\) (log) sum — the slide-2 move with \(f(\mathbf{d}_i\mid\btheta)=F_i^{y_i}(1-F_i)^{1-y_i}\).
  • Each observation contributes the probability of what actually happened — \(F_i\) if \(y_i=1\), \(1-F_i\) if \(y_i=0\). The MLE makes the observed string of 0s and 1s most probable.
  • Score = weight \(\times\) residual \((y_i-\hat F_i)\times\) regressor \(\mathbf{x}_i\) — the shape of \(\mathbf{X}'\be=\bzero\). The weight \(f/[F_i(1-F_i)]\) is signal \(f\) ÷ Bernoulli variance \(F_i(1-F_i)\): a GLS-style inverse-variance reweighting. The FOC is a residual orthogonal to \(\mathbf{x}\).
  • Logit cleans the weight: \(f=F(1-F)\Rightarrow\) weight \(1\), so \(\mathbf{s}_i=(y_i-\Lambda_i)\mathbf{x}_i\) — the OLS normal-equations shape. No closed form: glm fits it by IRLS (watch for perfect separation).

Part IV · Reading the estimates: marginal effects

A density gets in the way

Because the probability passes through \(F\), the effect of a continuous \(x_j\) is the coefficient scaled by the density at the index: \[\frac{\partial P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_j}=f(\mathbf{x}'\bbeta)\,\beta_j\ \neq\ \beta_j.\]

  • The marginal effect is \(f(\mathbf{x}'\bbeta)\beta_j\) — not \(\beta_j\).
  • Sign of \(\beta_j\) = sign of the effect (since \(f>0\)), but the magnitude depends on \(\mathbf{x}\) — largest near \(\mathbf{x}'\bbeta=0\) (\(P\approx\tfrac12\), where \(F\) is steepest), vanishing in the tails (where \(P\) is already \(\approx0\) or \(1\)).
  • This is why marginal effects, not coefficients, are the comparable, reportable quantity: a raw \(\hat\beta\) carries the model’s error-scale normalisation (Logit’s \(\approx1.6\times\) Probit’s), so their magnitudes aren’t comparable — but the density factor \(f\) rescales both onto the same footing.

MEM, AME, and the Delta method

Collapse the effect to one number — at the average individual (MEM) or averaged over the sample (AME) — and get its standard error with the Delta method, exactly as for nonlinear restrictions in Lecture 6: \[\text{AME}_j=\frac1n\sum_i f(\mathbf{x}_i'\hat\bbeta)\,\hat\beta_j, \qquad \widehat\Var\big(m_j(\hat\bbeta)\big)\approx \Big(\tfrac{\partial m_j}{\partial\bbeta}\Big)'\widehat\Var(\hat\bbeta)\Big(\tfrac{\partial m_j}{\partial\bbeta}\Big).\]

  • MEM \(=f(\bar{\mathbf{x}}'\hat\bbeta)\hat\beta_j\) (effect at the mean) vs. AME \(=\tfrac1n\sum_i f(\mathbf{x}_i'\hat\bbeta)\hat\beta_j\) (mean of effects) — they differ because \(f\) is nonlinear (Jensen). Report AME: the mean of real individuals’ effects, not an “average person” who may not exist.
  • SE via the Delta method — the same Taylor-then-variance move as the asymptotics (Part II): linearise \(m_j(\hat\bbeta)\) around \(\bbeta\), then sandwich the variance. The Lecture 6 tool, now closing the arc.
  • For a dummy regressor use a finite difference \(F(\cdots,1)-F(\cdots,0)\), not the derivative.

Part V · Synthesis

OLS, GMM, MLE: three criteria, one architecture

🔗Connection—The extremum-estimator table

Each row is defined by optimising a criterion (an M-estimator) and solved by setting an average to zero (a Z-estimator). The two middle columns are just those two faces of the same estimator.

Estimator Criterion — the M face FOC: an average \(=\bzero\) — the Z face Efficient when
OLS \(\min\ \tfrac1n\sum(y_i-\mathbf{x}_i'\bbeta)^2\) \(\mathbf{X}'\be=\bzero\) (residual \(\perp\mathbf{x}\)) errors homoskedastic (Gauss–Markov)
GMM \(\min\ n\,\bar{\mathbf{g}}_n'\bW\bar{\mathbf{g}}_n\) \(\bG'\bW\bar{\mathbf{g}}_n=\bzero\) (projected moments) \(\bW=\bS^{-1}\) (optimal weight)
MLE \(\max\ \tfrac1n\sum\log f(\mathbf{d}_i\mid\btheta)\) \(\tfrac1n\sum\mathbf{s}_i=\bzero\) (the score) always (Cramér–Rao), if correctly specified

Read it in two coordinates. Across — identical in every row: optimise a sample criterion \(\Rightarrow\) the FOC is “an average \(=\bzero\)” \(\Rightarrow\) the variance is a sandwich that collapses under the right metric. Down — what changes: which function is zeroed and how much of the distribution it needs — residual\(\times\)regressor needing only homoskedasticity (OLS), projected moments needing the optimal weight (GMM), the score needing the whole density (MLE). It is a ladder of efficiency vs. assumptions: each rung assumes more and, in return, squeezes out more information — MLE at the top (Cramér–Rao), paid for with correct specification.

What to take away

  • MLE is an extremum estimator: \(\hat\btheta=\argmax\tfrac1n\sum_i\log f(\mathbf{d}_i\mid\btheta)\) — defined by maximising (M-estimator) and solved by a zeroed average score (Z-estimator), the third member of the OLS/GMM family.
  • The score is a valid moment: \(\E[\mathbf{s}(\btheta_0)]=\bzero\), so the FOC \(\tfrac1n\sum_i\mathbf{s}_i=\bzero\) is a Z-estimator — MLE solves a moment equation.
  • Information equality \(\mathcal I=\E[\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}']=-\E[\bH]\) collapses the sandwich to \(\sqrt n(\hat\btheta-\btheta_0)\dto\Normal(\bzero,\mathcal I^{-1})\) — the Cramér–Rao bound, so MLE is efficient (if correctly specified).
  • Binary choice models \(P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x})=F(\mathbf{x}'\bbeta)\) (Logit/Probit); the score is a weighted residual \(\perp\mathbf{x}\), and only \(\bbeta/\sigma_u\) is identified — so coefficients are not comparable across links.
  • Report marginal effects, not coefficients: \(\partial P/\partial x_j=f(\mathbf{x}'\bbeta)\beta_j\), summarised as AME, with Delta-method standard errors.
